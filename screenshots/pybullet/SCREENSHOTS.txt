PyBullet 3D Simulation -- Screenshot Guide
===========================================

Six screenshots, each demonstrating a different capability of the
PyBullet port of this project's path-planning engine: four single-robot
scenarios driven by a Husky ground robot, plus the two multi-robot
demos (r2d2 robots) that put several of those single-robot pieces
together at once. legend.png is a standalone color/marker key meant
to sit alongside these on a poster or slide, since the in-simulation
HUD text is sized for an on-screen camera view and doesn't reproduce
well in print.


legend.png -- Legend
---------------------
Color and marker key for every element that appears across the three
scenarios below: path colors, terrain types and their cost multipliers,
obstacle states, and start/goal markers. Every swatch is pulled
directly from the simulation's own color constants, so it always
matches exactly what's on screen.


binary_vs_costmap.png -- Binary Obstacles vs. Cost-Map Routing
-----------------------------------------------------------------
A robot faces a single wall blocking the direct route to its goal.
Two paths are planned and drawn on the same map: a binary-obstacle
search that treats every non-obstacle cell as equally good (red, 29
cells, cost 28.0) and hugs the wall as tightly as geometry allows, and
a cost-map search that adds a soft "keep your distance" penalty near
obstacles (blue, 33 cells, cost 32.0), visible as the translucent
orange halo around the wall. The cost-map route accepts a slightly
longer path in exchange for real clearance -- the same tradeoff a
Nav2-style costmap inflation layer makes for a physical robot that
can't actually graze a wall the way a zero-radius grid search can.


terrain_routing.png -- Terrain-Aware Routing
-----------------------------------------------
The single wall is replaced with an actual forest floor: trees/rocks
(obstacles), bush, mud, and water scattered across the whole grid at
different densities, not one hand-placed patch -- the entire ground is
rendered in terrain color rather than just a painted square, so every
step of the route crosses genuinely uneven ground. A route that's
blind to terrain cost (red, cost 63.2) takes the geometrically direct
line through whatever bush/mud/water happens to be in the way; a
terrain-aware route (green, cost 51.4) actively threads between the
costlier patches instead, visibly diverging from the naive route in
several places rather than taking one single detour. Both routes still
respect real obstacles and keep clearance from them (the same
cost-map inflation used in binary_vs_costmap.png) -- terrain cost is
the only thing being compared here, not obstacle avoidance.


sensor_before.png / sensor_after.png -- Lidar-Limited Sensing & Replanning
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
A before/after pair showing what happens when a robot can only plan
against what it has actually sensed, not a perfect map. Real 3D
raycasts (not a simple radius check) reveal obstacles as the robot's
simulated lidar sweeps past them; anything not yet sensed stays
dimmed almost to invisibility, faithful to what the robot genuinely
doesn't know yet. The ground is now a scattered bush/mud/water forest
floor across the whole grid too (mirroring terrain_routing.png's "whole
grid, not one patch" treatment, and pygame's own hidden_animals.png/
unreliable_sensor.png) -- purely cosmetic here, since nav.sensor.KnownGrid
(what the robot actually plans against in sensor mode) never carries
terrain cost over from the real grid, so the route shown is exactly as
terrain-blind as it was before; only the rendered world changed.

  sensor_before.png: shortly after the robot's very first scan. Two
  nearby obstacle blocks are already visible and folded into its route;
  a third block -- squarely on the direct path to the goal -- is still
  outside sensor range and effectively invisible.

  sensor_after.png: moments later, the robot has driven close enough to
  confirm that hidden block. The discovery is marked with a gold "X" on
  the newly-confirmed cell, the old route is still visible fading out in
  gray, and the fresh replanned route flashes in bright magenta beside
  it -- a redraw that's actually visible frame to frame, not an
  instantaneous, easy-to-miss swap.

  Together, the pair is the core argument for sensor-limited planning:
  the "best" path changes as the world becomes better known, and a
  robot has to be able to notice that and react.


multi_robot_corridor.png -- Two Robots, One Corridor
---------------------------------------------------------
Four buildings leave a 3-cell-wide "plus" of open street through the
grid; robot A (cyan, north-south) and robot B (orange, east-west) start
on opposite streets and are spawned already aimed so their straight-line
routes would cross the center at the same moment. Every 0.05s both
robots replan symmetrically against the real grid plus a block placed
around the other robot's current cell -- so the detour visible here
(one path bending into the street's spare lane rather than driving
straight through) is a genuine, wall-verified A* replan on both sides,
not a steering nudge layered on top of a fixed plan. This frame is
captured right around the two robots' closest approach, which is where
that live avoidance is actually visible; a frame from earlier or later
in the run would just show one robot driving a straight line.
(pybullet_multi_robot_main.py)


cbs_intersection.png -- Four Robots, One Intersection (Conflict-Based Search)
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The same four-way intersection, but with all four compass arms in use
at once and no live replanning at all: every robot's entire route is
solved once, offline, as a single jointly conflict-free set of
time-indexed paths by nav/cbs.py's Conflict-Based Search, then driven
in lockstep (every robot advances to its next per-timestep waypoint
only once every robot has reached its current one, so the fleet's
simulated positions always correspond to one shared CBS timestep).
Captured partway through the shared route, with all four color-coded
paths visible and every robot near the shared crossing at once -- the
actual claim CBS makes is about the whole joint plan, not any one
robot's leg of it. (pybullet_cbs_main.py)
